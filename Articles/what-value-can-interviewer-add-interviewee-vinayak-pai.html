What value can an interviewer add to an interviewee

Created on 2015-04-10 15:35

Published on 2015-04-10 15:35

As any senior person in an IT organization, I end up doing lot of interviews. While a part of my role requires me to evaluate prospective candidates, once I am convinced of an individual’s capabilities I also need to do selling. I need to show what value my organization can add to that individual. I am also the ambassador of my organization and should represent my organization powerfully in order to influence the candidate to join us.

 It is imperative that I find ways in which the interviewee perceives value added to him/her through the conversation. This would help improve my company’s brand image in the market as well as increases the chances of the person joining us.

 Here are a few things I generally attempt

 If it is a women candidate, I highlight my organization’s focus on women and how my organization was voted as the best place to work for women.

 I also share my observations about the candidates which are not controversial in nature, like communication or better of way presenting their experience or inconsistencies in their profile. Most of the candidates are happy to take this feedback and work on them. Most of them have told me that generally this does not happen in an interview.

 I also give tips on how to make the profile more presentable. This works well with junior candidates however this might not work with everyone.

 I also end the conversation asking for feedback about myself and my organization and if I learn about anything that has gone wrong, apologize for that. This ensures that if they had had a bitter experience in the entire process, they speak it out and feel better.

 One thing which works with almost everyone is the career discussion, what is hot in the market, what are the skills they need to pick up in the months ahead so that they are prepared for the world ahead

 Here is a sample of what that discussion would look like

 Most of the people I interview are working in the area of Data Warehousing and Business Intelligence. Some of them would have exposure to Analytics and/or big data.

 I start explaining my companies view on the “Data Analytics Continuum”, Descriptive Analytics -> Predictive Analytics -> Prescriptive Analytics -> Cognitive Analytics?

 I leave a question mark around Cognitive Analytics because that is a highly debated topic currently.

 I talk to them about Data Science and the importance of Analytics and Data Science. I talk about how Coursera and Khan academy are revolutionizing the learning area. I talk how Stanford and MIT are not far behind and have free eLearning courses.

 I talk to them about Big Data, and the developments in that area and depending on the seniority of the people, it becomes an interesting conversation

 I talk to them about research reports from Gartner and Forester. Many a times I find senior people who wouldn’t have heard of a Gartner Magic Quadrant and they find it very interesting and have thanked me for telling them about it.

 I talk to them about other learning from different sources, talk about a TED video where MIT’s Deb Roy speaks about how he recorded over 250,000 hours of video and audio of his baby son’s life – categorizing and analyzing nearly every word heard by and, eventually spoken by his son, providing an accurate map of how one learns to speak and reason. I also encourage them to view it. I talk about the advance data visualizations demoed in the video and how they are going to play a major role in the future of Data Analysis.

 I talk about SMAC and Internet of Things and then when the person is fully excited tell the story of how my organization is playing a role in the journey of internet of things as the world’s first Bluetooth smart 4.2 IP provider.

 This generally ends the conversation on a high note.

 While all of this takes just 15 minutes, more often than not, the person walks away with a unique feeling that they wouldn’t have had before.
