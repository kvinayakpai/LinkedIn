When the Balloon Pops: Building a Career That Can Survive Change

A career is rarely a straight line. It is more often a sequence of experiments, detours, abrupt endings, and unexpected doors. Nagesh Pai’s own path crosses engineering, marketing, mutual funds, digital banking, teaching, customer support, software engineering, and independent consulting. He jokingly calls the result “Mumbai ki bhelpuri”: an unconventional mixture that makes sense only when viewed as a whole.

That story matters now because the anxiety surrounding artificial intelligence is not really about a new tool. It is about permanence. We want a degree to guarantee a role, a title to guarantee relevance, and a company to guarantee continuity. But technology has never offered those guarantees. What it can offer is a new way to think about change.

The balloon is temporary. Your capacity to serve is not.

One of Pai’s most memorable mental models is the balloon parable. A child delights in a balloon and then cries when it bursts. The parent enjoys the same moment but knows from the beginning that the balloon will not last. A resilient professional must hold both perspectives at once: play wholeheartedly while the opportunity is in hand, and remember that the opportunity is temporary.

“As long as the balloon is in your hands, use it fully and ask what service you can render to yourself and others. And like the parent, keep in mind that nothing is forever.” — Nagesh Pai

This is not detachment from work. It is a healthier form of commitment. Give the role your energy without allowing the role to become your identity. When a project ends, when a company restructures, or when a familiar skill loses value, the question changes from “Why did this happen to me?” to “Where is my service needed next?”

Treat AI as an auzaar (Tool/weapon), not a god

Pai explains AI through the carpenter’s power saw. A hand saw may take 30 minutes to make a cut that a power saw completes in five. The power saw does not erase carpentry. It changes the economics of the work and raises expectations of speed. The carpenter who understands the material, the measurement, and the finished object remains essential. The carpenter who refuses the new instrument becomes less competitive.

The same distinction applies to knowledge work. AI can accelerate research, organize documentation, suggest alternatives, and generate a first draft. It does not remove the obligation to understand, test, verify, and take responsibility. “Auzaar ki tarah istemal karo, bhagwan mat banao”: use it as an instrument; do not turn it into a god.

Knowledge is the enduring asset

Tools have short half-lives. Knowledge compounds. Pai connects this to Amrit Manthan, the churning of the cosmic ocean: wealth, recognition, difficulty, and reward emerge as by-products, but the deeper value lies in the churning itself. For a craftsperson, the daily “kick” is seeing something new emerge from practice.

This reframes career development. The goal is not to predict the one tool that will dominate the next decade. The goal is to build judgment that transfers across tools: curiosity, fundamentals, communication, financial discipline, and the ability to turn knowledge into useful outcomes for others.

Three questions to carry into the next transition

  • What service can I render with the opportunity currently in my hands?

  • Which part of my craft is fundamental, and which part is merely a tool or interface?

  • What am I learning now that will still matter after this role, platform, or technology changes?
